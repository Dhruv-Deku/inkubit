Inside a circuit

Verification says whether a run is right. These tools show why: the state after every gate, who is entangled with whom, which noise source costs the most, whether a shorter circuit does the same job, and whether any classical explanation could fake the correlations.

Gate by gate

After every run, or before it with Step through, Inkubit computes from the specification alone the exact state after each gate. It shows the amplitudes, with phases relative to the largest amplitude (a global phase is unobservable), the state in Dirac notation, and a Bloch sphere per qubit. An arrow shorter than the sphere's radius means that qubit is entangled with others. The spheres are drawn in 3D and share one camera, so turning one turns all of them and they stay comparable.

Entanglement is drawn as a web. Each entangled pair gets a gold thread whose width is its concurrence (Wootters' formula, tested against Qiskit's), and each qubit's gold arc is its entanglement entropy. Group entanglement in which no pair is entangled on its own, as in a GHZ state, is drawn as a dashed hull and named, because concurrence alone would show nothing.

The same view carries the engineering numbers. The error budget gives the fidelity each noise source costs when it acts alone. A sweep finds how much better the hardware must be for a 99% match (a Bell pair needs about 7×). Readout correction inverts each bit's known confusion matrix. The chip cost compiles the circuit onto all-to-all, line and heavy-hex wirings and counts the extra SWAPs. These noisy analyses evolve the density matrix with the verifier's own error channels, built in closed form, and a test checks that they agree with Qiskit Aer to 1e-8. A what-if slider scales the noise from perfect to ten times worse and shows the exact distribution at each position, not an interpolation.

Shorter circuits, proven equal

Find a shorter circuit runs up to four optimisers: rewrite rules, the Qiskit transpiler at level 3, the two together, and (live) Muse Spark. Each rewrite rule comes with a reason a student can check:

  • H·H = 1; S then S is Z; H on both sides turns CX into CZ;
  • a CNOT whose control is still |0⟩ never fires; a CZ whose partner is |1⟩ is a Z on the other qubit (phase kickback);
  • a diagonal gate right before measurement changes nothing a measurement can see; a SWAP before measurement is a relabelling of classical bits; gates that never reach a measured qubit can go.

No proposal is trusted. prove() compares each one with the original exactly and labels it with the strongest claim that holds: the same gate for every input (process fidelity, up to 8 qubits), the same state from |0…0⟩, or the same measured odds. Anything else is rejected, together with the outcome where it differs. The thresholds are floating-point tolerances, not physics: 1 − F < 10⁻¹², and a distance below 10⁻⁹ for the odds. A fuzz test applies the rules to 300 random circuits and requires every rewrite to keep the odds; 4,000 more passed locally.

Muse Spark sees the circuit, the measured qubits, its cost and the best cost so far, but never another optimiser's circuit. If its proposal is rejected, it gets one retry with the exact reason. On the two-qubit Grover example, whose answer is fixed in advance, Muse Spark noticed that the circuit only ever produces |11⟩ and proposed two X gates. Exact simulation proved it, where rewrite rules plus Qiskit's level-3 transpiler had stopped at seven gates. When a result only flips bits, the panel says so: the circuit's answer was fixed in advance. Phase estimation of a known phase, for example, shrinks from 15 two-qubit gates to one X gate. That is true and worth knowing, but it is not a trick that works for unknown inputs.

Zero-noise extrapolation

Error mitigation lets today's noisy machines estimate what a perfect one would give. Inkubit first inverts readout error, then runs the circuit at 1×, 3× and 5× its gate noise by folding: U, then U U⁻¹ U, then U U⁻¹ U U⁻¹ U. These have the same ideal result with three and five times the gates, and folding is what hardware can actually do. Every outcome's probability is then extrapolated back to zero noise with Richardson weights (15/8, −5/4, 3/8). With exact probabilities, a Grover search goes from a 0.882 match as measured to 0.999. The panel repeats the method with 4,000 sampled shots per noise level, because extrapolation amplifies shot noise: it needs about 5.2 times the shots for the same error bars. For some circuits that cost eats the gain, and the panel says so.

Bell tests and tomography

Is it really quantum? For every entangled pair, Inkubit computes the CHSH value under the best measurement settings, from the Horodecki closed form S = 2√(m₁ + m₂) of the pair's correlation matrix. Classical theories allow at most 2, and quantum mechanics reaches 2.83. When exactly three qubits are entangled, it also computes the Mermin value: classical theories allow at most 2, quantum mechanics 4. That value has no closed form, so it is optimised from 16 fixed starts, and the panel says the settings were found, not proven optimal. Each value is computed three ways: ideal; on the noisy state with the settings planned for the ideal one, as an experiment would do; and from 4,000 sampled shots per setting, with readout error and a standard error.

  • A Bell pair gives 2.828 ideal and 2.395 ± 0.025 on realistic hardware, 16 standard errors above the classical limit.
  • A GHZ state has no entangled pair at all, yet reaches the Mermin maximum of 4.
  • A W state's pairs are entangled but stay within the classical limit (1.886), while the trio reaches 3.046, the known value.

State tomography rebuilds the state the way a laboratory must, for up to three qubits. It measures along X, Y or Z in all 27 combinations (1,000 shots each, readout error included and then inverted), estimates every Pauli expectation, inverts to a density matrix and projects it onto the nearest physical state (Smolin, Gambetta and Smith, 2012). The result is drawn as two 3D cityscapes, the intended state against the rebuilt one, with the fidelity, the purity, and how much of the gap is hardware noise and how much is finite shots.

The Quantum Rosetta

Quantum computing is written in several languages at once: Dirac notation, circuit diagrams, and five or more programming languages. Newcomers often stall on the notation before they reach the physics. The Rosetta translates between all of them, both ways, and checks every translation instead of trusting it.

  • Reading is exact wherever a grammar exists. A recursive-descent parser reads Dirac notation (implicit products, ⊗, √, π, eiφ, named states and simple LaTeX). Qiskit's importers read OpenQASM 2 and 3, and small line parsers read Quil and Qiskit gate calls. Muse Spark is used only for code without a parser here (Cirq, PennyLane, Q#, Braket) and for photos, which it transcribes under a schema with an instruction not to solve. The transcription then goes through the exact parser, and model readings are labelled.
  • A state gets a circuit from the cheapest of several methods: one rotation per independent qubit, a CNOT fan-out for GHZ-like groups, a breadth-first search over textbook gates for groups of up to three qubits, Qiskit's state preparation, or a gallery circuit that makes the same state. The result must reach fidelity 1 − 10⁻⁸ by exact simulation, or the request fails.
  • Eight output languages are written by fixed rules. The Qiskit, OpenQASM 2 and 3 and Cirq versions are executed and must give the same state, and the page says which were run.

Every step's sentence ("entangles qubits 0 and 1", "changes only a phase") is derived from the exact states before and after it, not generated by a model.

Labs and games

Chemistry, cryptography and optimisation from first principles, and games that teach by being scored exactly. In each one the model helps, and exact physics keeps score.

Challenges

Eleven puzzles in three levels (warm-up, entanglement, gate golf) ask the player to build a target state or gate: a fair coin, a minus sign, a quarter turn, NOT without X, a Bell pair, a singlet, a hidden phase, GHZ, SWAP from CNOTs, CZ from one CNOT, and the W state. Each puzzle is defined by a reference circuit that never leaves the server. State puzzles compare |⟨target|ψ⟩|² and gate puzzles compare the process fidelity |Tr(Ut†U)|²/d², so a global phase never counts and any correct construction is accepted. Solved means a fidelity of at least 0.999. Stars work like golf (solved, within par + 2, at or under par), and each reference solution is tested to reach exactly par.

Near misses get specific feedback, such as "the probabilities are already right; a relative phase is off" or "2 of 4 basis inputs already map correctly". Muse Spark gives hints: it sees the goal, the player's circuit, the exact score and the author's hint for that stage, never the solution, and it names at most one gate. A puzzle can be solved by clicking gates onto wires, by describing the answer in words, or by drawing it on paper and taking a photo.

Bug hunt

One gate in a known circuit is wrong: a wrong wire, control and target swapped, a look-alike gate, an angle slip or a stray gate. The player compares what the circuit should give with what it gave, and clicks the culprit against the clock. Rounds are stateless: (level, seed) regenerates the same round, so the page never receives the answer before the player guesses. Each planted bug must move at least 20% of the probability, so it can be seen in 1,000 shots.

Grading is by exact search, not by matching the planted index. Every one-gate edit (delete it, swap the gate, move it, flip control and target, change the angle) is simulated, and any gate whose edit restores the intended odds exactly counts. Some circuits have many such gates: in Shor's circuit, 22 of 28 gates counted. A round is therefore re-planted when too many gates would count. Live, Muse Spark plays the same round in parallel, from exactly what the player sees. In checks it found the bug in 4 of 4 rounds, in 9 to 24 seconds.

Split the party (QAOA)

Guests and feuds form a graph the player edits by clicking. QAOA seats the guests at two tables, which is the Max-Cut problem, the shape of many scheduling and routing problems. Brute force gives the true best seating; the depth-1 landscape shows every angle pair with the optimiser's path; depths 1 to 3 are optimised exactly. Checked: the 6-ring at depth 1 gives exactly ¾ of the best cut, the known value, and depth 3 solves it. Three layers find a best seating 95% of the time with perfect qubits and about half the time with realistic noise. The summary says plainly that at this size QAOA shows the mechanism, not a speedup.

H₂ from first principles

The molecule lab computes the hydrogen molecule without a chemistry package. The STO-3G integrals for two s functions have closed forms (Szabo and Ostlund, chapter 3), and they are tested against the book's values. By symmetry the problem reduces to one qubit, H = aI + bZ + cX, and the page says so. VQE measures Z and X under the realistic preset's noise; since that noise maps the Bloch vector affinely, three simulations fix every angle's expectation (checked to 10⁻⁵), taking the lab from 110 s to under 4 s.

At 2.5 Å, where the electrons become correlated, Hartree-Fock is off by 233 mHa. Raw VQE on realistic noisy hardware is off by 21 mHa. Undoing the calibrated noise map, which is what calibration circuits measure on hardware, brings it to 0.8 mHa from the exact energy, against a shot-noise error bar of 2.1 mHa at 4,000 shots. The mitigated value lands within shot noise of exact at all 28 bond lengths.

Catch the spy (BB84)

Quantum key distribution with an eavesdropper. Random bits and bases, an intercept-resend eavesdropper on a share f of the photons, sifting, the error-rate check and privacy amplification run photon by photon, following the measurement rule (checked against Qiskit). Eve on a share f adds f/4 to the error rate, estimated from half the sifted bits. Above 11% the key is thrown away; below, it shrinks to n(1 − 2h(QBER)), so what remains is what Eve cannot know. At f = 1 she guesses 75% of the bits, and both facts are checked statistically.
