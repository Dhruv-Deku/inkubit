Evidence

Claims about AI and quantum hardware are easy to make and hard to check. This chapter collects the measurements behind Inkubit's design: how far a model alone can be trusted, what happens when two models debate, how good a learned shortcut is, and how the predictions held up on a real quantum computer.

AI alone vs Inkubit

The pitch that "a chatbot hallucinates and Inkubit does not" was tested, not asserted. The same model ({{ar_model}}, medium reasoning effort) was given each circuit as data, with no title, no notes and no simulator. It predicted every outcome's probability under a JSON schema, the way a chat assistant would answer. Exact simulation scored each answer by total variation distance, the share of probability in the wrong place: exact below 1%, close below 5%, wrong from 5%. The sets were the textbook examples, which a model has seen many times; new seeded circuits of 3 to 5 qubits plus a six-guest QAOA circuit, which is what a student's own sketch looks like to a model; and bigger new circuits of 6 to 8 qubits, all put in superposition first, with 4 measured. The recorded run is dated {{ar_date}}.

The first run disproved the easy version of the claim: the model was exact on {{ar_tb}} textbook circuits and on {{ar_new}} new small ones, and the page says so. It failed where the calculation gets hard. It gave no usable answer for the 31-gate QAOA circuit. On the bigger circuits it was exact on {{ar_big}}, close on {{ar_big_close}}, wrong on {{ar_big_wrong}}, and gave no answer on {{ar_big_none}}. The wrong answer was confident: it described every outcome as equally likely (1/16 each, "a near-maximally mixed marginal") and put {{ar_worst}}% of the probability in the wrong place. Nothing in its wording signalled doubt.

The AI referee

If one model can be wrong, can a second model catch it? The referee tests this. Muse Spark answers three questions about a circuit. Muse Wonder agrees with or disputes each answer, giving its own value. Muse Spark then stands by its answer or concedes. Exact simulation grades every position: probabilities within 0.02, the most likely outcome with ties allowed, and entanglement by the negativity of the two-qubit reduced state.

A first version let Muse Spark choose its own claims. It chose safe ones and was right on 3 of 3 every time, which tests nothing. The referee therefore now asks the same three questions of every circuit: the most likely outcome, the probability of the second most likely one (picked from the exact odds; the models are not told why) and whether a given pair of qubits is entangled. The test used the six bigger circuits from the AI-alone benchmark and two smaller new ones, {{ref_n}} answers in all.

{{table:referee}}

Debate helped on balance: {{ref_first}} right before, {{ref_final}} after. But it also added errors of its own. Muse Wonder raised {{ref_false}} false alarms against right answers and talked Muse Spark out of {{ref_talked}} of them, and it let {{ref_missed}} of the {{ref_wrong}} wrong answers through. Twice the two models agreed that a pair of qubits was entangled when its negativity was exactly zero (docs/REFEREE.md). A debate between models cannot tell which challenge was right; only the simulator can. That is why Inkubit checks instead of debating.

The ML verifier

Exact density-matrix simulation is the referee everywhere in Inkubit, but it costs time that grows exponentially. The ML verifier is a fast first opinion: it predicts how much of a circuit's answer survives realistic noise (the Hellinger fidelity against the ideal distribution, at a noise scale from 0.05× to 5×), with a calibrated error bar.

  • Inputs (16): structure (qubits, measured qubits, gates by size, depth, two-qubit depth, the busiest qubit), the ideal distribution (entropy, largest outcome, outcomes above 10⁻³), the noise scale, the rule of thumb (the product of gate and readout success rates), and two physics-informed inputs computed on the ideal distribution alone: exact readout errors, and a global depolarizing model. Those two were added after the first model was systematically optimistic on textbook circuits. Structure alone cannot see that a Bell pair's outcomes are two bit flips apart, so that every readout error leaves the answer.
  • Data: {{ml_samples}} random circuits of 1 to 7 qubits in five styles (gate soup, layered, few gates, mirror circuits with certain answers, reversible logic), labelled by exact density-matrix simulation ({{ml_exact_ms}} ms each on average); {{ml_train}} for training, {{ml_cal}} for calibration and {{ml_test}} held out.
  • Model: an ensemble of five small tanh networks (16-48-48-1), trained in NumPy with Adam. The ensemble mean is the prediction. The interval is adaptive split conformal: the error divided by the ensemble's spread (plus 0.01), calibrated at the 90th percentile.
{{table:ml}}

About {{ml_speedup}} times faster than the exact label. The coverage guarantee holds for circuits like the training set. That is why every prediction up to 10 qubits is still checked exactly, and why a miss is shown as a note about the model, never as a change to the result.

A real IBM quantum computer

Everything above is simulation. To test the simulations against reality, scripts/hardware_check.py ran four circuits (a Bell pair, GHZ-3, Bernstein-Vazirani and two-qubit Grover) on {{hw_device}}, the least busy device on the IBM Quantum Platform's Open plan (job {{hw_job}}, {{hw_shots}} shots each, {{hw_date}}, done 10 seconds after submission). The circuits were transpiled at optimisation level 3, which left one or two two-qubit gates each. The measured counts were compared, by total variation distance, with four predictions:

  • the noiseless odds;
  • Inkubit's generic realistic preset, the default noise model of the whole app;
  • a digital twin: Qiskit Aer with NoiseModel.from_backend, built from the device's own calibration data and run on the same transpiled circuit;
  • Inkubit's calibrated preset, added after this run: its own noise model with the median of a calibration snapshot of the same device (below).
{{table:hardware}}

The digital twin predicted what the chip measured more closely than the noiseless odds and the generic preset on {{hw_best}} of {{hw_n}} circuits, at distances of {{hw_twin}}. The finding that matters more for the app is the second one. The generic preset ({{hw_generic}}) was further off than the noiseless prediction on {{hw_pessimistic}} of {{hw_n}} circuits: it is calibrated to an older, noisier class of chip, and this one is better.

That finding became a feature. scripts/calibration_snapshot.py reads the device's reported calibration and keeps the median over its {{cal_qubits}} qubits and couplers: on {{cal_device}} ({{cal_family}}), a two-qubit error of {{cal_2q}}, one-qubit error {{cal_1q}}, readout errors of {{cal_p01}} and {{cal_p10}}, T1 {{cal_t1}} µs and T2 {{cal_t2}} µs, against the generic preset's 1.5%, 0.1%, 2% and 4%. A reported gate error already includes decoherence during the gate, so the thermal part is taken out before the rest becomes depolarizing noise; a test checks that each gate's composed error equals the reported one. The result is a fourth preset, Calibrated, in the run bar and in every analysis. Rescored on the stored counts, it came within {{hw_cal}}, closer than the twin on {{hw_cal_beats}} of {{hw_n}}. That is still above the {{hw_noise}} that shot noise alone would leave, so part of the chip's behaviour is unmodelled. Two cautions are stated in the app: the snapshot was taken a few hours after the run, and medians describe a typical qubit rather than the best patch a compiler picks.

What this does and does not show

Four small circuits, with one or two two-qubit gates each, on one device on one day. They show that Inkubit's pipeline reaches real hardware, and that a calibrated twin predicts it well at this scale. They are not a benchmark of the device. An offline rehearsal (--fake, on a simulated backend) checks the pipeline in CI and is never shown as hardware data. A test refuses to quote hardware numbers from a rehearsal.

AI-discovered circuits on the chip

The discovery lab's claims (Chapter 7) were made in simulation. scripts/discovery_hardware.py reruns the lab's search for three goals and sends each goal's best proven circuit to the chip, together with the baselines it beat: {{dh_circuits}} circuits on {{dh_device}} (job {{dh_job}}, {{dh_shots}} shots each, {{dh_date}}). GHZ-7 runs on seven physical qubits wired like a 7-qubit Falcon chip ({{dh_patch}}, picked by Qiskit's noise-aware layout for the discovered circuit). Every variant is compiled onto that same patch, so the textbook circuit pays the SWAPs the lab counted: {{dh_text_2q}} two-qubit gates on the chip against {{dh_found_2q}}.

Computational-basis counts cannot tell a GHZ state from a classical 50/50 mixture of all-0 and all-1, so the GHZ circuits also measure a parity oscillation over eight phases. The fidelity estimate is F = (P(0…0) + P(1…1))/2 + |coherence|, with error bars from shot noise; both are tested against exact states and against repeated sampling. A value above ½ certifies genuine 7-qubit entanglement.

GHZ-7: {{dh_src}} reached F = {{dh_found}} ± {{dh_found_se}} (populations {{dh_found_pop}}, coherence {{dh_found_coh}}) against {{dh_text}} ± {{dh_text_se}} for the textbook circuit, {{dh_z}} standard errors apart. Both certify genuine 7-qubit entanglement. Qiskit's generic state preparation, {{dh_prep_2q}} two-qubit gates, kept an outcome fidelity of {{dh_prep_outcome}}. W and Dicke: compiled freely for the chip, the discovered circuits used {{dh_w_found_2q}} two-qubit gates against {{dh_w_base_2q}}, yet their measured odds tied with Qiskit's within shot noise ({{dh_wz}} and {{dh_dz}} standard errors). Computational-basis odds cannot see phase errors, which is much of what fewer gates save, and the page reports a tie, not a win. The digital twin was optimistic about every circuit in this run (it predicted {{dh_twin_found}} for the discovered GHZ circuit) but ranked them in the same order.

IBM Quantum: placement and teleportation

The app now maps {{ix_devices}} IBM Heron chips qubit by qubit from their own calibration, and an advisor scores every way to place a circuit on them: each embedding of the circuit's two-qubit gates in the chip's working couplers, ranked by the chance that no gate or readout fails. Two experiments in one job on {{ix_device}} ({{ix_job}}, {{ix_circuits}} circuits) tested what the app now claims.

Placement matters. The advisor scored {{ix_candidates}} placements of a 5-qubit GHZ chain from a snapshot taken minutes earlier. The best, median and worst ran on exactly those qubits: GHZ fidelity {{ix_best}} ± {{ix_best_se}}, {{ix_med}} and {{ix_worst}}, in the advisor's order (its scores: {{ix_best_score}}, {{ix_med_score}}, {{ix_worst_score}}). The best and the worst placement were {{ix_z}} standard errors apart, and on the worst qubits the state no longer certifies entanglement. Same circuit, same chip, same hour; only the qubits differed.

Teleportation with real feed-forward. This run came first, from a script; feed-forward is now part of the app's format and verifier as well (decision D83), and the gallery's feed-forward teleportation sends the same if_test circuit to the chip from the editor. A qubit was teleported with the Bell measurement in the middle of the circuit and X and Z applied by the chip in real time, only when the measured bits said so (qubits {{ix_tele_qubits}}). The six axis states form a 2-design, so their average fidelity equals the average over all states: {{ix_tele}} ± {{ix_tele_se}}, against 2/3 for the best classical scheme, and {{ix_tele_none}} without the corrections, as theory says.

The proof sheet: every check on every circuit

A run is verified by one comparison (Chapter 5). The proof sheet asks more, of the circuit and of Inkubit itself: {{ps_checks}} independent checks of {{ps_kinds}} kinds, each guarding against a different mistake. Two ideal simulators (Qiskit's quantum_info and Aer's C++) and two noisy ones (closed-form channels and Aer's density matrix) must agree. The noisy state must be a physical density matrix. The circuits Qiskit compiles for three chip wirings must be the same unitary, ancillas and routing included: translation validation of the compiler. Exports to OpenQASM 2 and 3 must read back to the same circuit. Sampled shots must pass the shot-noise bound and a G-test. The generated script must verify in the sandbox. Renaming the qubits, or inserting gate pairs that cancel, must leave the answer unchanged (metamorphic tests). It runs on any circuit from the editor in about eight seconds.

Over the whole gallery ({{ps_circuits}} circuits) it recorded {{ps_passed}} passes and {{ps_failed}} failures, {{ps_skipped}} check skipped with its reason and {{ps_notes}} notes, where the ML verifier's interval missed the exact value, consistent with its coverage on textbook circuits. A test breaks Qiskit's compiler on purpose (dropping one CNOT) and requires the compilation check to fail. Its first run also found a real defect: Inkubit's OpenQASM 2 export used sx, cp and swap, which the standard qelib1.inc does not define, so strict readers rejected it. The app's own read-back had used Qiskit's lenient mode and hidden this. The export now defines those gates in the file from standard ones.
